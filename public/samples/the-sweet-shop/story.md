# Chapter 1: The Ledger

The sweet shop on Bidhan Sarani had been called Haldar & Sons for sixty years, though there had never been any sons, only Mitali's grandfather, and now only Mitali.

She opened it the morning after the cremation because Dadu would have been furious if she hadn't. The shutter rattled up, the smell of cardamom and burnt sugar rolled out into the street, and the brass bell over the door gave its cracked little ring. Dadu used to say the bell rang only for honest customers, and that was why it rang so rarely.

The ledger was where he had always kept it, under the counter beside the weighing scale with the dented tin pan. It was a fat red book swollen with damp, every page in his tiny Bengali hand. Mitali turned to the last entry and found the problem at once: a page had been torn out, cleanly, along the spine. The entries jumped from March to May as if April had never happened.

At ten o'clock Mr. Sen came in with his umbrella still dripping. He owned the building, and his father had owned it before him.

"My condolences," he said, not sitting. "Your grandfather was behind on the rent for eleven months. I was patient for his sake. I won't be patient for yours. You have until Durga Puja."

When he had gone, her cousin Bablu slid in from the back lane, all apology and wet hair. He had borrowed four thousand rupees from Dadu in the spring for a business that had failed in the summer.

"I'll pay it back by Puja," he said. "Every paisa. I swear on his photograph."

Mitali looked at the photograph on the wall, garlanded with marigolds, and did not say what she thought.

# Chapter 2: Two-Thread Syrup

The rain did not stop for three days. College Street turned into a brown river, and nobody came for sweets.

Mitali spent the time doing the one thing Dadu had taught her properly: syrup. He had never let her use a thermometer. "Listen to it," he would say, and he made her stand over the big kadhai with her eyes closed until she could hear the difference between one-thread syrup, which sounded like rain on a tin roof, and two-thread, which sounded like someone tearing silk. Two-thread was for sandesh. One-thread was for rasgulla. Get it wrong and the whole batch was ruined.

On the third night the power failed. Mitali was alone in the shop with a kadhai on the gas and water already lapping at the front step. She heard a car stop outside, then the heavy knock of men who wanted something.

"Sen-babu says to collect the scale and the vessels," one of them called. "Collateral."

The front shutter had always jammed. You had to lift it from the left corner, never the middle, or it stuck halfway and could not be moved up or down for an hour. Dadu had cursed it every morning for forty years. Mitali had cursed it too.

Now she crossed the shop in the dark, took hold of the shutter in the very middle, and pulled it down hard. It screeched and jammed solid, a hand's width above the floor.

The men outside heaved at it, swore, heaved again, and finally drove away. Mitali sat down on the wet floor and laughed until she cried, while behind her the syrup ticked over from one-thread to two.

# Chapter 3: Puja

The sweet competition at the Simla Street pandal was the biggest in North Kolkata. The prize was fifteen thousand rupees and a framed certificate signed by the club president.

Mitali made sandesh. On the morning of the judging, the boy she had hired to help turned the flame too high, and the syrup started to race. She closed her eyes. Rain on a tin roof, then silk tearing. She pulled the kadhai off the heat at exactly the right moment, before the boy had even noticed anything was wrong.

The sandesh won.

She carried the certificate back to the shop that evening and, because there was nowhere else to put it, set it on the counter beside the old weighing scale. The dented tin pan wobbled. Mitali lifted it to straighten it, and underneath, folded small and flat, was a page torn from the red ledger.

April. In Dadu's tiny hand: *Lent to N. Sen, son of the landlord, for his daughter's wedding: 1,60,000 rupees. To be set against rent. No interest. He is a good boy.*

Eleven months of rent came to one lakh thirty-two thousand.

When Mr. Sen came the next morning, Mitali did not say anything at all. She simply turned the ledger page around on the counter so he could read it. He read it twice. Then he took off his glasses, put them back on, and quietly folded his umbrella.

Bablu arrived at noon with an envelope. Four thousand rupees, in old notes that smelled of mothballs. It was the first time in his life he had kept a promise on time. Mitali put the money in the till and, for once, did not say what she thought about that either.

Above the door, the brass bell hung silent all day.
