# Chapter 1: The House on Salt Lane

Ines Varga came to her father's house on the last ferry of October, with one suitcase and the firm intention of selling it. The estate agent had given her three weeks. Three weeks seemed generous for a house that had waited twenty years for her to come back to it.

The front door stuck, as it always had. Inside, everything smelled of pipe smoke and cold tea. Her father had been dead for nine days, and nothing in the hall had noticed.

She walked through the rooms with her coat still on. The armchair had kept the shape of him. On the mantel stood a photograph of the two of them on the quay, her face turned away from the camera and toward the water. She had not known he kept that one. She turned it face down, then turned it back.

She put her case down and looked at the kitchen clock. It was eleven minutes fast. It had been eleven minutes fast her whole childhood, because her father had set it that way on purpose and held to the habit like a creed. "The clock keeps me honest," he used to say. "Eleven minutes is the difference between a man who catches the boat and a man who waves at it."

In the study, the oak desk stood where it had always stood, under the window that looked out at the water. Two of its drawers slid open at a touch, full of string and receipts and dried-out pens. The bottom drawer was locked. The brass key to it had hung round her father's neck on a faded green ribbon for as long as she could remember, and he had never let anyone open that drawer, not her mother, not Ines, not once. The undertaker had handed her a small envelope at the funeral home. In it were his watch, his wedding ring, and the key on its ribbon.

She turned the key over in her palm. It was warm from her pocket and smaller than it deserved to be. She wasn't ready to decide what it was for, so she put it back in her coat.

In the hall cupboard, behind a row of wellingtons, she found the old flare gun, its orange barrel chalky with salt. Her father had kept it loaded for emergencies on the water, though in forty years of crossing the channel he had never once had an emergency. She shut the cupboard on it and went to find clean sheets.

That night the house ticked and settled around her. Somewhere in the walls, the clock went on being eleven minutes ahead of everyone.

# Chapter 2: Mrs Okafor's Soup

Mrs Okafor arrived at nine the next morning, carrying a pot with a tea towel over it, as though the soup might catch cold on the way across the lane. She was seventy-eight and had lived next door for fifty years. She looked Ines up and down, said, "You're thinner than your photographs," and walked into the kitchen without being asked.

"I've been watering his geraniums," she said, lifting the lid. "Somebody had to. He'd have haunted me."

Ines sat at the table where she had done her homework as a child. The soup was lentil, and better than anything she had eaten in the city in a year.

"He talked about you," Mrs Okafor said.

"He had a funny way of showing it."

"He had a funny way of showing everything." The old woman sat down opposite and folded her hands. "You know what he did every evening, at dusk? He walked down to the harbour wall and whistled. Three notes, always the same. Down, down, up. Never a whole tune, only those three. I asked him once who he was calling. He said, 'Whoever's listening.'"

"That sounds like him," Ines said, and found that her throat had gone tight.

"Forty years, that was. Rain or shine." Mrs Okafor shook her head. "I never did find out what answered."

After she had gone, Ines washed the bowls and stood at the window over the sink. Beyond the roofs she could see a thin grey strip of the channel, and her stomach did what it always did at the sight of open water. She had not stood on a boat's open deck since she was nine, the summer she had gone over the side of the pilot launch and her father had hauled her out by her collar, coughing and furious and white to the lips. She took the ferry now only because there was no other way off the island, and she always sat inside, in the middle, away from the windows, with her eyes on a magazine she did not read.

She had told herself for years that the fear was a small thing, a childhood bruise. But the strip of grey in the window looked very large this morning, and she drew the curtain across it.

She spent the afternoon making lists of what could be sold. The lamps, the armchair, the barometer in the hall. At the end of the list she wrote desk, and then, after a while, she crossed it out.

# Chapter 3: The Drawer

On the third night she gave up pretending she would sleep. At two in the morning she came downstairs in her father's old cardigan, switched on the study lamp, knelt in front of the desk, and took the green ribbon out of her pocket.

The key went into the lock as though it had been waiting. It turned with a dry click that she felt in her teeth.

The bottom drawer was full of envelopes. She counted them by the lamplight: twenty, tied in bundles with kitchen string, each one addressed in her father's square capitals to Miss Ines Varga, at an address in the city. None had a stamp. On the back of each, in pencil, was a date. It took her a moment to understand that every date was her birthday.

She opened the first. It was one page long. He wrote that the house was quiet, that the geraniums had done well, that he hoped she was eating properly. He wrote that he did not know how to post a letter to a daughter who had not asked for one. He signed it only, Dad.

She read all twenty before the window went grey. They were not sad, exactly. They were short and stubborn and full of weather, tide tables and small repairs, the news of a man who had decided to go on talking to someone who could not hear him. In the twelfth he wrote about the afternoon on the pilot launch.

"I have never told you," he wrote, "that I was more frightened than you were. I held you by your collar and thought that if my hand opened, I would not be able to forgive it. You stopped going on deck after that. I let you stop. It is the thing I would take back, if I were given one."

Ines sat on the floor with the letter in her lap and the cold coming up through the boards. Outside, the first gulls were arguing over the harbour.

At the very bottom of the drawer lay one last envelope, tied with the same green ribbon as the key. It was blank. Inside was a single line.

"Go and see who answers."

She read it three times. Then she folded it into the cardigan pocket and went to put the kettle on. The kitchen clock said ten past six, which meant it was not yet six, and the whole day was still in front of her.

# Chapter 4: Whoever's Listening

For three days she cleaned. She scrubbed the stove, beat the rugs on the line, and opened every window until the smell of pipe smoke thinned and the house began to smell of salt and bleach and wet stone, like a place where someone might live.

She did not sell the house that week. She did not call the agent. She spent the days reading the tide tables in the margins of her father's letters, and on the fifth evening, a little before dusk, she put on her coat and walked down to the harbour wall.

The wind came sideways off the channel and the water slapped at the stones below her. She stood where he must have stood, hands deep in her pockets, and felt foolish. Then she pursed her lips and whistled. Down, down, up.

Nothing. A rope clanked against a mast. She whistled again, louder.

Out in the dark water, a long grey head rose, and then another. Seals, six or seven of them, rolling their wet eyes at her with the patience of a congregation. They had been waiting for him, and she was not him, but they came in anyway, close enough to hear her laugh and then, to her surprise, to hear her cry.

On the morning of the ferry her watch had stopped in the night. She did not mind. She packed the letters in her case and went by the kitchen clock instead, which said thirty-six minutes past four. The boat left at half past. She ran the whole length of Salt Lane with her case banging against her knee, certain that she had missed it, and came round the corner of the quay to find the ferry still tied up and the ramp still down and the deckhand lazily coiling rope. The clock was eleven minutes fast, as it always was, and for once she was grateful to it.

The deckhand tipped his cap to her as she passed. "Miss Varga," he said. "Sorry about your dad. He was on this boat every Thursday for forty years, and not once did he sit outside, either." She did not know what to say to that, so she nodded and held on to her case.

She boarded and stood in the passage by the cabin door, as she always did. The engines shuddered. Through the glass she could see the open deck, wet and bright and empty, and the rail beyond it, and beyond the rail the whole wide, glittering, terrifying channel.

She put her hand on the door.

It was heavier than she remembered. The wind took her hair and her breath at once. She walked out, one step and then another, until her fingers closed round the cold rail, and she stood with her knees shaking and watched the island draw back from her, small and green and full of her father's tide tables. She did not go over the side. Nothing happened except the crossing, which was all there had ever been to it.

Halfway across, the ferry captain came down from the bridge, took off his cap, and told her that he was her long-lost half-brother and that he had been waiting years to say so. He handed her a deed to the cottage beside the harbour. She said thank you. It seemed the least she could do.

When the ferry reached the mainland she was still standing at the rail, and she stayed there until the last rope was thrown.
